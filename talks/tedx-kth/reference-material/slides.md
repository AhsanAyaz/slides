## Muhammad Ahsan Ayaz | Stockholm | May 4, 2026 | First speaker

### Published title (TEDx-approved, on the event site):
### *The next billion developers won't be blocked by syntax. They'll be blocked by thinking.*

### Signature line (the closing beat, spoken-only, no slide):
### *"The typing has been automated. The thinking has not."*

The two work together. The published title is the prediction. The signature line is the same idea distilled, delivered after the audience has watched you earn it. Title is 14 words; signature line is 9. The compression itself signals authority.

## What changed from v2

Five surgical revisions, each anchored to a specific storytelling principle. The macro structure is unchanged. The arc, the stories, the close, the sources, all preserved.

1. **Branded the asymmetry as "the thinking gap."** The single biggest stickiness gain in the talk. The audience now has a portable name for the concept they will remember and quote. Slide 7 gets a label.
2. **Added a loop opener before the asymmetry reveal.** Resets attention right before the talk's emotional pivot and frames the asymmetry as a secret the speaker almost missed.
3. **Added two "narrate their thoughts" moments.** One after the ADK reveal, one after the asymmetry numbers. Both at points where the audience would otherwise be silently questioning the speaker. Beating them to the question builds intimacy and trust.
4. **Tightened three unintentional hedges.** "I think it is happening in many other industries too" became "it is happening in yours." Same content, embedded truth instead of qualifier. Deliberate vulnerability hedges (the close especially) preserved.
5. **One sharper negative frame.** The syntax-as-trap passage in Act 5 now lands as a warning, not an observation.

What was NOT changed (and the reasons):
- **No villain added.** A manufactured villain (AI, Big Tech, the industry) would compete with Maja Bosnic's structural-injustice arc and read as cynical to the KTH audience.
- **No "ends in control" Hollywood resolution.** The unresolved close is the talk's reason to exist. Control passes to the audience via the generational handoff, which is a more sophisticated version of the same tip.

## The Full Script v3

*(approximately 2,820 words; ~20:00 at TEDx pace with indicated pauses)*

*(Ahsan walks to the red dot. Doesn't smile yet. Holds for two seconds. Looks at the floor, then up.)*

I want to tell you about four hours of my life I will never get back.

*(Beat. Not apologetic, just direct.)*

A few months ago, I was preparing a demo for a tech conference. Real conference, real stage, real engineers in the audience who would smell a fake from the back row. I wanted to demo something called Google ADK; the Agent Development Kit. Specifically, an agent doing RAG... retrieval augmented generation. If you don't know what that means, it doesn't matter. What matters is, I wanted it to look impressive.

So I decided I would not just run it on my laptop. I would build a beautiful web interface. I would deploy the front end to Firebase Hosting. I would put the back end on Google Cloud Run. I would cross-wire permissions across two Google Cloud projects. I would set up service accounts. I would make it production grade. Why?

*(Beat.)*

Because I am a senior engineer, and that is what senior engineers do.

*(Slight knowing smile. Walk one step.)*

And I had AI helping me the entire time. Claude Code. Cursor. Copilot. The full orchestra. Every time I hit an error, the AI generated the fix. Every time the fix broke something else, the AI generated the next fix. Permission denied. Service account misconfigured. Cross-project access blocked. Token expired. Generate, paste, deploy, wait, fail. Generate, paste, deploy, wait, fail.

*(Hands moving like keystrokes.)*

Four hours. I am not exaggerating. By hour four, it was past one in the morning. I was on my sixth coffee of the day. Two of the smartest models on Earth and a senior engineer with twelve years of experience, locked in a beautiful, syntactically perfect death spiral.

*(Stop. Long beat.)*

And then I sat back in my chair, and I realized something so simple it physically hurt.

I did not need any of this.

*(Quieter.)*

For the demo, I just needed to type three letters and a word into my terminal. ADK web. That's it. Local agent, service account file, talking to the data, working perfectly. I had been solving a problem I did not have. The AI had been very, very helpfully solving that problem with me. Together we had built a magnificent answer to a question nobody asked.

*(Pause.)*

**[NEW: narrate their thoughts]** And I know what some of you are thinking right now. *How does a senior engineer waste four hours like that?* Believe me, I asked myself the same question for a week afterwards. The answer is exactly what this whole talk is about.

*(Pause. Look directly at the audience.)*

The bottleneck was never the code. It was never the syntax. It was never the deployment. The bottleneck was that I had not stopped, for four hours, to think clearly about what I was actually trying to do.

*(Beat.)*

That is the moment this whole talk hinges on. Hold onto it. We are coming back.

---

But I owe you honesty, so let me also tell you the other side. Because I am not standing here to tell you AI is bad. AI has, genuinely, given me superpowers.

*(Tone lifts. Walk again.)*

A few months before the demo, my blog got hacked. Years of writing, gone. All my subscribers, gone. No backups, no snapshots. The kind of moment where you stand in your kitchen and quietly consider becoming a goat farmer.

I recovered everything. With AI. Walked through the database, reconstructed the data, repaired the schemas. A week of work, done in an evening. *That* is real.

I migrated that whole blog from a content system that was costing me money I did not want to spend, to one based on plain text files. Six dollars a month, gone. Tiny number. Felt like sovereignty.

I wrote a book this year. A whole book. In three months, while working a full-time job, while traveling for conferences, while being a husband and a person. AI did not write it for me. To be clear. But AI took the friction out of the parts of writing that are not writing. The thinking was still mine. The output came out the door three times faster.

And I built a marketing agent on Telegram. I named it Alkhwarizmi.

*(Smile. This one matters.)*

In the 9th century, in Baghdad, in a place called the House of Wisdom, there was a Persian mathematician named Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi. He worked on algebra, on astronomy, on geography. When his work was translated into Latin, his name got mangled into a single word. That word is *algorithm*. Every algorithm you have ever used, every recommendation engine, every search result, every trading bot, every model you have ever prompted, all of them carry his name. He has been working for you your whole life and you didn't know it.

*(Pause. Let it land.)*

I named my marketing agent after him because I am a developer who cannot do marketing. I have never been able to do marketing. I find it genuinely hard. So I built an agent and I let it teach me, in messages on my phone, how to position my work, how to write hooks, how to think about an audience. It has been, frankly, more patient with me than most humans.

*(Self-deprecating beat.)*

And it works. It actually works. I have shipped more in the last twelve months than in the previous five years combined. I have products. I have a book. I have agents. I have systems. I have a whole catalog of small, quiet pieces of software that solve real problems.

*(Stop. Drop the voice.)*

**[NEW: loop opener]** And honestly, that should be the end of the talk. AI helped me ship more than I have ever shipped, the future is bright, thank you, good night.

*(Beat.)*

But that is not why I am standing here. Because there is something underneath all of that I did not see for almost a year. And once I saw it, I could not unsee it.

---

*(Pause. This is the turn.)*

Most of those products do not have a hundred users.

*(Beat.)*

Some of them have nine.

I built them faster than I have ever built anything in my life. They are clean. They work. And nobody is using them.

*(Don't rush. Let it breathe.)*

But there is one thing I have built that is not like that. It's a community platform. It's called codewithahsan.dev, and right now, as I'm standing here, just under four thousand developers are using it. There are mentors and mentees on it. There are people whose careers have measurably changed because of it.

*(Quieter.)*

And here is what is strange. AI helped me build that one too. Every line of the database. Every screen. Every notification. The same models. The same hands. The same year.

*(Pause.)*

**[NEW: narrate their thoughts]** Now you might be thinking what I thought, the first time I noticed the gap. *Okay, the four-thousand-user one was just a better idea. He got lucky on one and unlucky on ten. That's how it goes.*

That is what I told myself for months. It is not true.

*(Pause. Then quieter.)*

**[NEW: branded term]** I started calling this *the thinking gap*. It is the difference between the things I built and the things people use. And it has nothing to do with the AI. The AI is the same on both sides of it.

*(Long beat.)*

The ten that nobody uses, I built fast. I had an idea, I shipped a thing, I moved on. The AI was so helpful that I never had to stop. I never had to sit with a question. I never had to ask whether the thing should exist, who it was for, what they actually needed, whether anyone was waiting for it. The friction was gone. So the thinking was gone too.

The one that worked, I built slowly, even with all that speed available to me. Because I had been in that community for years before I built it. I knew what people needed. I knew what they kept asking me. I knew the shape of the problem before I wrote a line.

*(Pause.)*

The AI was not what made the difference. The AI was the same in both cases. What was different was whether I had crossed the thinking gap before I started typing.

---

*(Walk slowly. The dragon section. Hold it heavy.)*

There is something happening right now in our industry, and **[CHANGED: tightened hedge]** it is happening in yours too, whatever you study. I have not figured out what to do with it yet, but I want to name it.

The price of building has collapsed. A nineteen year old, this weekend, in Stockholm or Karachi or anywhere, can ship a real product. A designer with no engineering background can prototype an idea in an afternoon. A non-native English speaker can write professional documentation that opens doors that were closed to them. That is not nothing. That is, genuinely, a kind of opening up. The gate to building software has, in my lifetime, never been lower.

But the gate got kicked open at the same moment something else started shifting. Stack Overflow ran their developer survey last year. Eighty four percent of developers say they use, or plan to use, AI tools. Up from seventy six percent the year before. That's the line everyone repeats.

Here is the line they don't. In the same survey, the developers who *trust* the tools dropped to twenty nine percent. Down from forty.

*(Let that sit.)*

We are using AI more, and trusting it less, and shipping more from it, and many of the things we are shipping are not finding their people. Use up. Trust down. Output up. Demand for any one piece of output, down.

I am not saying this to make you afraid. I am saying it because it is what is actually happening, and we should talk about it honestly rather than pretend it isn't.

*(Beat.)*

Because if everyone can build, the question stops being *can you build*. The question becomes *should this exist*. And that is a much, much harder question than the one I spent the first decade of my career learning to answer.

---

So I want to come back to that demo. The four hours I lost. Because that story is not just funny. It is the entire thesis of what I want to leave you with tonight.

*(Walk to centre. Slower.)*

Here is what is actually going on. The AI was not failing me in those four hours. The AI was doing exactly what I asked. That's the trap. It is so good at executing on a request that it will execute on the wrong request, beautifully, for as long as you let it. It will not stop and say, "Ahsan, do you actually need cross-project permissions for a five minute demo?" It will just generate the next config file.

The thing AI cannot do for you, the thing it has not learned to do, the thing I am not sure it can ever fully do, is decide what problem is actually worth solving in the first place.

Andrej Karpathy, who runs in much higher circles than I do, said recently that the limiting factor in software is no longer coding skill. It is clarity of thought. He said the developer is becoming an orchestrator. Someone who specifies. Who reviews. Who decides. The typing has been automated. The thinking has not.

And that is the part where I get a little hopeful, and a little scared, at the same time.

*(Beat.)*

Because if the thing that is valuable now is the thinking, then the question for every single person in this room, no matter what you study, is the question I have been asking myself.

Are you actually thinking? Or are you just typing faster?

*(Long pause.)*

A lot of what I did in those four hours was typing. Confident, expensive, sophisticated typing. None of it was thinking. The thinking would have taken thirty seconds. *What does the demo actually need?* That is the entire question. I never asked it.

There is a phrase emerging right now in my world: *context engineering*. It sounds technical. It is not, really. Context engineering is just the practice of being very, very deliberate about what you put in front of an intelligent system before you ask it to do anything. What does it know? What is the goal? What are the constraints? What does success look like? Because if you do that work clearly, the system gives you something useful. And if you don't, it gives you four hours of perfectly generated garbage.

But that's not just true of AI. That has always been true of *people*. Of teams. Of design. Of everything any of us has ever tried to do well. The only difference is, before, you could survive on syntax. You could survive by being the person who knew the language. **[CHANGED: sharper negative frame]** Now, syntax is free. The work that used to hide behind syntax... taste, judgment, the willingness to stop and ask "wait, what am I actually doing"... that is the part the AI cannot do for you. And if you skip it, the speed becomes the trap. The AI will help you get to the wrong answer faster than you have ever gotten there in your life.

*(Quieter.)*

That is the inheritance Al-Khwarizmi left us, by the way. He didn't just give us algorithms. He gave us the idea that you can think a problem clearly enough that the steps to solve it become obvious. The Arabic word that became *algebra* is *al-jabr*: completion, balancing, putting the broken parts back together. The thinking is the work. It always was. We just spent forty years hiding it behind a wall of syntax, and now the wall is coming down.

---

*(Steady. Direct.)*

I am the first speaker tonight, and there are extraordinary people coming after me. You will hear about how innovation gets funded. You will hear about who gets a voice in the systems being built around us. I am not here to tell you what to think about any of that. I am here, as the developer who got to sit in front of these tools first, to tell you what I have learned the hard way.

The tools are going to keep getting better. They are going to keep removing more of the work I used to do. And in the next few years, every single person in this room is going to face some version of the question I faced at one in the morning, on my sixth coffee, fighting with permissions for a problem that did not exist.

The question is not *can I build this*. The answer to that is yes. The answer to that, increasingly, is yes for everyone.

The question is *what is worth building*.

*(Pause.)*

And I want to be honest with you, because you deserve honesty in this room: I do not know how to answer that question yet. I have shipped ten things that almost nobody wants, and one thing that thousands of people use, and I am still learning the difference. I think it has something to do with sitting with a problem long enough that you actually understand it. I think it has something to do with caring about the people you are building for more than you care about the thing you are building. I think it has something to do with being willing to *not* ship, sometimes, even when you can.

But I am not done figuring it out.

*(Walk back to centre.)*

---

So I want to leave you tonight not with an answer, because I do not have one. With a question. The same question I am sitting with.

*(Beat. Look up.)*

If the typing has been automated... if anyone in this room can build almost anything by next weekend... then what, exactly, should we be building? Who is it for? Why does it deserve to exist?

That is your generation's question, more than it is mine. Most of the things that will matter in the next thirty years have not been built yet. Some of you will build them. The tools will not stop you. They will not even slow you down.

But they also will not tell you what is worth building. That part is still ours.

*(Beat.)*

The typing has been automated. The thinking has not.

*(Half-smile. One nod. Step back from the dot. Don't bow. Just walk.)*

Thank you.

## Storytelling-tip evaluation: what each tip got us

For each of the nine tips you shared, here is what made it into the talk and why.

**1. Center on unexpected change. Cause and effect.**
*Already strong in v2.* The four-hour demo story is a perfect orienting-response trigger. Tightened the connective tissue between the ADK story and the thinking-gap reveal so the cause-and-effect chain is one continuous line instead of two.

**2. Moral outrage. Heroes vs villains.**
*Resisted.* No villain added. The talk's conflict is Self vs Self (Three Great Conflicts). A manufactured villain would compete with Maja Bosnic's structural-injustice arc and read as cynical. Kept the internal-honesty register.

**3. Show a struggle that ends in control.**
*Half-applied, intentionally.* The ADK story ends in control (the `adk web` reveal). The talk as a whole refuses control, but transfers agency to the audience via the generational handoff. That is a more sophisticated version of the same principle.

**4. Brand your key terms.**
*Fully applied.* Added "the thinking gap" as the spine name. Slide 7 carries the label. The audience now has a portable concept they can quote, screenshot, and repeat.

**5. Speak using embedded truths.**
*Selectively applied.* Tightened "I think it is happening in many other industries too" to "it is happening in yours too." Tightened "I am not saying this to make you afraid. I am saying it because I think it is what is actually happening" to "I am saying it because it is what is actually happening." Preserved the deliberate hedges in the closing ("I do not know how to answer that question yet") because they are the talk's emotional currency.

**6. Narrate their thoughts.**
*Fully applied at two key moments.* (a) After the ADK reveal: "I know what some of you are thinking right now. How does a senior engineer waste four hours like that?" This catches the audience's silent skepticism and turns it into agreement. (b) After the asymmetry numbers: "Now you might be thinking what I thought, the first time I noticed the gap. Okay, the four-thousand-user one was just a better idea." This intercepts the most likely dismissal of the asymmetry before it can take root.

**7. Utilize negative frames.**
*Applied to the syntax-trap passage.* The line now ends with: "And if you skip it, the speed becomes the trap. The AI will help you get to the wrong answer faster than you have ever gotten there in your life." Same content as before. Now it threatens.

**8. Drop in loop openers.**
*Fully applied.* New passage right before the asymmetry reveal: "And honestly, that should be the end of the talk. AI helped me ship more than I have ever shipped, the future is bright, thank you, good night. But that is not why I am standing here. Because there is something underneath all of that I did not see for almost a year." This resets attention right before the talk's emotional pivot and frames what follows as a secret.

**9. Use contrast words.**
*Already heavy in v2.* No additional changes needed. "But," "actually," "instead," "turns out" appear throughout.

## Final delivery notes

Three things to watch in rehearsal:

**1. The branded term needs to land cleanly, not as a soundbite.** When you say "I started calling this the thinking gap," say it like you actually started calling it that, not like you are trying to coin a phrase on stage. The audience will smell the difference. Own it as a working name you've been using privately for months.

**2. The narrate-their-thoughts moments need a specific delivery rhythm.** Pause before saying what you imagine the audience is thinking. Then say it slightly faster than the surrounding text, slightly higher in pitch, almost in their voice. Then drop back to your normal pace for the answer. The voice change is what makes it feel like you are reading their mind rather than putting words in their mouth.

**3. The loop opener needs to feel almost casual.** "And honestly, that should be the end of the talk" works only if you sound like you mean it. If it sounds rehearsed, the audience hears a setup and the trick fails. Practice it like you are confiding in a friend that you almost stopped writing the talk at this point.

Good luck.
