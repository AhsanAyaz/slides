~ 7:00 [Reveal this slide as you say his full name.] In the 9th century, in Baghdad, in a place called the House of Wisdom, there was a Persian mathematician named Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi. Every algorithm you have ever used, every recommendation engine, every search result, every trading bot, every model you have ever prompted, all of them carry his name. Context engineering
Be deliberate about what you put in front of an intelligent system before you ask it to do anything.
~ 15:30 There is a phrase emerging right now in my world: context engineering. It sounds technical. It is not, really. Context engineering is just the practice of being very, very deliberate about what you put in front of an intelligent system before you ask it to do anything. What does it know? What is the goal? What are the constraints? What does success look like? Because if you do that work clearly, the system gives you something useful. And if you don't, it gives you four hours of perfectly generated garbage. But that's not just true of AI. That has always been true of people. Of teams. Of design. Of everything any of us has ever tried to do well. The only difference is, before, you could survive on syntax. You could survive by being the person who knew the language. Now, syntax is free. The work that used to hide behind syntax — taste, judgment, the willingness to stop and ask "wait, what am I actually doing" — that is the part the AI cannot do for you. And if you skip it, the speed becomes the trap. The AI will help you get to the wrong answer faster than you have ever gotten there in your life. [Quieter.] That is the inheritance Al-Khwarizmi left us. He didn't just give us algorithms. He gave us the idea that you can think a problem clearly enough that the steps to solve it become obvious. The Arabic word that became algebra is al-jabr: completion, balancing, putting the broken parts back together. The thinking is the work. It always was. We just spent forty years hiding it behind a wall of syntax, and now the wall is coming down. [Steady. Direct.] I am the first speaker tonight, and there are extraordinary people coming after me. You will hear about how innovation gets funded. You will hear about who gets a voice in the systems being built around us. I am not here to tell you what to think about any of that. I am here, as the developer who got to sit in front of these tools first, to tell you what I have learned the hard way. The tools are going to keep getting better. They are going to keep removing more of the work I used to do. And in the next few years, every single person in this room is going to face some version of the question I faced at one in the morning, on my sixth coffee, fighting with permissions for a problem that did not exist. The question is not can I build this. The answer to that is yes. The answer to that, increasingly, is yes for everyone. The question is what is worth building. [→ ADVANCE to slide 12]
